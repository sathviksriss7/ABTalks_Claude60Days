Concept · Headline Accuracy

Headlines are written to be clicked — not to be complete.

A headline's job is to grab attention in under two seconds. The article's job is to be accurate. Those two goals often pull in opposite directions — which is why a headline can be technically "based on" a story while still badly misrepresenting it.

In everyday life, this is the gap between the headline you see while scrolling and the actual substance underneath — the gap most people never check before sharing.

Concept · Emotional Manipulation

If a post makes you feel something fast, ask why.

Fear, outrage, urgency, and envy spread faster than calm explanation — algorithms and advertisers know this. Content is often engineered to trigger a feeling before your critical thinking has a chance to catch up.

This shows up everywhere: ads that make you feel behind, posts that make you feel afraid, captions that make you feel like everyone else already knows something you don't.

    🧭 Three habits to build

    Small, repeatable checks that make you much harder to manipulate:

    1

    Read past the headline. Before reacting or sharing, read at least the first three paragraphs — that's where the nuance and caveats usually live.

    2

    Notice the feeling, then pause. A sudden spike of fear, anger, or urgency is often the point of the content, not a side effect. Treat that spike as a cue to slow down.

    3

    Ask who benefits. Who wrote this, who's paying for it, and what do they gain if you click, share, or buy? Follow the incentive, not just the claim.
