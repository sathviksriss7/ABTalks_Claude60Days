    Module 01Foundational

    Measures of Central Tendency

    Every dataset needs a single number that answers "what's typical here?" That number is a measure of central tendency. There are three main candidates — mean, median, and mode — and picking the wrong one is one of the most common statistical mistakes people make.

    1.1 — The Mean (the balance point)

    The mean (arithmetic average) is the sum of all values divided by how many values there are. It's the point where the dataset "balances" if every value were a weight on a see-saw.

    Mean formulax̄ = ( Σx ) / n
    ⚖️
    Analogy — the see-saw. Imagine every data point as a person sitting on a number-line see-saw at their value. The mean is exactly where you'd place the fulcrum so the see-saw balances perfectly. Move one person far to the right (an outlier), and the whole fulcrum has to shift toward them — that's why the mean is outlier-sensitive.
    Each dot is a quiz score. The fulcrum sits at the mean — the balance point of the whole see-saw.

    1.2 — The Median (the middle person in line)

    The median is the middle value once all data is sorted. If n is odd, it's the exact middle value; if n is even, it's the average of the two middle values.

    🚶
    Analogy — line up by height. Ask a class to physically line up from shortest to tallest. The median is whoever is standing exactly in the middle. It doesn't matter if the tallest person is 6'2" or 8'0" — the middle person doesn't move. That's why the median resists outliers.

    1.3 — The Mode (the crowd favorite)

    The mode is the value that appears most often. It's the only measure of central tendency that works on categorical (non-numeric) data, like "favorite flavor."

    🍕
    Analogy — the pizza order. If a class votes on pizza toppings, the mode is whatever topping got the most votes — you can't "average" pepperoni and mushroom, but you can say which one is most popular.

    Module 02Building up

    Measures of Spread (Dispersion)

    Two classes can both average 15/20 on a quiz — but in one class everyone scored 14–16, and in the other scores ranged from 3 to 20. Central tendency alone hides that difference. Spread tells us how tightly or loosely data clusters around the center.

    2.1 — Range

    The simplest measure: the distance between the maximum and minimum values.

    Range formulaRange = Max − Min

    2.2 — Variance & Standard Deviation

    Variance measures the average squared distance of each point from the mean. Standard deviation is just its square root — brought back into the original units, which makes it far easier to interpret.

    Sample variances² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
    Sample standard deviations = √s²

    Why n − 1, not n?

    When we only have a sample of a bigger population, dividing by n slightly underestimates the true spread. Dividing by n − 1 (called Bessel's correction) corrects that bias, giving a fair estimate of the population's real variance.

    🎯
    Analogy — darts on a dartboard. The mean is the bullseye. Standard deviation is roughly "how far, on average, each dart lands from the bullseye." A tight cluster of darts near the center = low std dev. Darts scattered all over the board = high std dev.
    Deviations (red lines) from the mean are squared, averaged, then square-rooted to get standard deviation.

    2.3 — Quartiles & the Interquartile Range (IQR)

    Quartiles split sorted data into four equal chunks. Q1 (25th percentile), Q2 (the median, 50th percentile), and Q3 (75th percentile). The IQR is the width of the middle 50% of the data — and it barely notices outliers.

    Interquartile rangeIQR = Q3 − Q1

    ✕
    Misconception: "Range is a reliable measure of spread." Range uses only two data points — one wild outlier can double it overnight, while every other value stays put.

    Key takeaways

    • Range is fast but fragile — only two points determine it.
    • Variance/std dev use every point; std dev is in original units.
    • Sample variance divides by n − 1 to correct for bias (Bessel's correction).
    • IQR ignores the extreme 25% on each end — the outlier-resistant choice.

    Module 03Applying it

    Distribution Shape

    Two datasets can share the same mean and std dev, yet look completely different when plotted. Shape — symmetry, skew, and how "normal" the data looks — is the missing piece that turns numbers into a real picture.

    3.1 — Symmetric vs. Skewed

    A distribution is symmetric when the left and right halves mirror each other (mean ≈ median). It's skewed when a long tail stretches out to one side, dragging the mean away from the median.

    🐘
    Analogy — the tail tells the direction. Think of the "tail" as literally an elephant's tail: a right-skewed distribution has its tail pointing right (a few huge values, like millionaire incomes in a room of average earners). A left-skewed one has its tail pointing left (a few very low values, like a handful of students who scored near zero on an otherwise easy quiz).
    In right skew, the mean is pulled above the median by the long right tail — and vice versa for left skew.

    3.2 — The Normal Distribution & the Empirical Rule

    Many natural datasets cluster into a symmetric "bell curve" — the normal distribution. The empirical rule (68-95-99.7 rule) describes how data spreads around the mean in a normal distribution.

    68% of data falls within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3.

    3.3 — Z-scores

    A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean — a universal "how unusual is this?" ruler that works across any dataset.

    Z-scorez = (x − x̄) / s
    📏
    Analogy — steps from home base. If the mean is "home base" and the standard deviation is the length of one stride, the z-score just counts how many strides away a value stands — and in which direction.

    3.4 — Spotting Outliers (IQR method)

    A common rule: any value more than 1.5 × IQR below Q1 or above Q3 is flagged as a potential outlier.

    Outlier fencesLower fence = Q1 − 1.5×IQR  |  Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5×IQR

    Key takeaways

    • Right skew: mean > median. Left skew: mean < median. Symmetric: mean ≈ median.
    • The empirical rule (68-95-99.7) only applies to roughly normal distributions.
    • Z-scores standardize values so you can compare across different datasets.
    • The IQR method (1.5×IQR fences) is a simple, robust way to flag outliers.

    Visualization & Putting It All Together

    Numbers describe a dataset; pictures make it click instantly. This module covers choosing the right chart and combining everything you've learned into one real analysis.

    4.1 — Choosing the Right Chart

    ChartBest forWatch out for
    HistogramDistribution shape of continuous numeric dataBars touch (no gaps) — it's showing continuous ranges, not categories
    Bar chartComparing categorical data (categories, counts)Bars have gaps; order is often arbitrary
    Box plotComparing spread & outliers across groups at a glanceHides the actual shape of the data within each quartile
    ✕
    Misconception: "Histograms and bar charts are interchangeable." Histogram bars touch because the x-axis is a continuous number line; bar chart bars are separated because categories have no inherent numeric order.

    4.2 — Anatomy of a Box Plot

    A box plot is built directly from the five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum.

    The box spans the IQR (Q1–Q3); whiskers reach to the furthest non-outlier points; dots mark flagged outliers.

    Summary Notes

    Module 1 — Central Tendency: Mean, median, and mode each answer "what's typical" differently. Mean is precise but outlier-sensitive; median is robust; mode is the only categorical option.

    Module 2 — Spread: A single "typical" value hides how consistent or variable the data is. Range is fast but fragile; variance/std dev use every point; IQR is the robust middle-50% measure.

    Module 3 — Shape: Comparing mean and median reveals skew direction. The empirical rule and z-scores let you judge how unusual a value is, and the 1.5×IQR rule gives a repeatable way to flag outliers.

    Module 4 — Visualization: Choose histograms for shape, bar charts for categories, box plots for comparing groups — and let skew guide whether you report mean+std dev or median+IQR.
